He Had the Blessing. So Why Was He Sleeping on a Stone?

Parashat Vayetzi 5787 · First Aliyah · Bereshit 28:10–22

7 min readThe WAY Derech Ha Yashar Ministries

Yaakov left Be'er Sheva carrying his father's blessing. By nightfall he was lying on bare ground somewhere on the road to Haran, with a stone by his head for a pillow.

That picture has always bothered me a little, and I think it should. The man who carries the promise given to Avraham is also a man on the run. Esav has said he will kill him once their father is dead. Rivkah has sent him off to her brother until things cool down. The family is split down the middle, and nobody can say when Yaakov will come home, or whether he will.

We tend to talk about the promises of יהוה as if receiving one ought to smooth out the road. Yaakov's life doesn't fit that idea at all, and Vayetzi opens right in the middle of the tension: a covenant promise over his life and the open sky over his head.

The blessing came with a history

You can't read Bereshit 28 honestly while skipping Bereshit 27. Yaakov deceived his father. Rivkah planned it with him. Esav's fury is the reason Yaakov is on this road at all.

The Torah doesn't ask us to overlook the deception because Yaakov happens to be in the covenant line. I'd also warn against the opposite mistake, which is to treat every hardship he meets afterwards as a punishment handed down from heaven. The text says neither. What it gives us is harder to hold: a man whose choices have real consequences, and a promise that still stands.

Our failures don't make יהוה unfaithful to His word, and His faithfulness doesn't turn our failures into righteousness. If you like a tidy moral ledger, with good people getting good things and bad people getting bad things, Yaakov is awkward company. He has received a blessing and still has a great deal to learn.

I suspect that's why so many of us recognise ourselves in him. We know what's right and still make choices that hurt the people around us. We talk about trusting יהוה and then grip the outcome with both hands. We're given something precious and handle it carelessly. Having received a blessing settles very little on its own. The harder question is what kind of people we are becoming while we carry it.

An ordinary place to stop

Bereshit 28:11 tells us Yaakov came upon a certain place and stayed the night there because the sun had set. The Hebrew keeps returning to one word, ha-makom, “the place”. It appears three times in that single verse, yet nothing about the spot seems to matter to Yaakov except that it's where the dark caught him. He takes one of the stones lying there, puts it by his head and lies down.

Then he dreams. A sullam, a stairway or ladder, is set on the earth with its top reaching towards heaven, and messengers of Elohim are going up and coming down on it. The order is ascending first, then descending. People have built whole sermons on that detail. The text doesn't explain it, and I'm not going to pretend it does. The scene is remarkable enough without help.

Yaakov thought he had simply stopped for the night. He woke up to find that heaven had not been absent from the ground he slept on. His words were:

Bereshit 28:16

“Surely יהוה is in this place, and I did not know it.”

He isn't saying that יהוה turned up during the night. He's admitting that he hadn't noticed. Those are two very different things. The gap was in Yaakov's own awareness, and the dream closed it.

I want to be careful here, because this verse often gets stretched. It doesn't teach that every hard situation hides a private revelation, or that every uncomfortable experience is a coded message from heaven. It tells us something specific about Yaakov at this place. Still, it leaves a fair question for the rest of us. How often are we so taken up with what has gone wrong that we stop seeing the instruction, the responsibilities and the opportunities already in front of us?

What יהוה actually said

The ladder gets most of the attention. Was it a staircase? What does it represent? Why are the messengers travelling in both directions? Those are worthwhile questions, but they aren't the centre of the passage. The centre is what יהוה says.

He names Himself the Elohim of Avraham and Yitzchak. He renews the promise of the land and of descendants, and says the families of the earth will be blessed through Yaakov and his offspring. Then He speaks to Yaakov personally: “I am with you.” He promises to guard him wherever he goes, to bring him back to this land, and not to leave him until He has done what He has spoken.

There's no promise in any of that that the coming years will be easy. He doesn't say Lavan will deal fairly with him, or that his household will be spared jealousy and grief. As we keep reading Vayetzi this week, we'll watch every one of those troubles arrive.

Some of us were taught to read the faithfulness of יהוה off our circumstances. If things go well, He must be pleased; if they go badly, something must be wrong. That idea wouldn't last a week in Lavan's household. A covenant promise doesn't guarantee that people will treat you well, and it doesn't excuse you from acting responsibly either. Yaakov will still have to work, make decisions, put up with injustice and answer for his own conduct. What he carries away from Beit-El is the word spoken to him there, and he'll need it in the years ahead.

Same stone, different meaning

In the morning Yaakov takes the stone he'd put by his head, stands it up as a pillar and pours oil on top of it. He names the place Beit-El, the House of Elohim.

I find this one of the most striking details in the whole passage. Overnight the stone was nothing more than something to lay his head beside. By morning it was a memorial. It was the same rock. What had changed was what Yaakov knew when he looked at it, and from then on he had a fixed point for remembering what יהוה had promised him.

This is where it gets personal. Most of us have an excellent memory for what people have done to us. We can replay the betrayal, the broken promise, the humiliating conversation, the day things fell apart, and years later we can still quote it almost word for word. I wonder whether we remember the instruction of יהוה half as clearly. Could we say what He has already told us in His Word? Could we show that we're living by it?

It's possible to put so much energy into remembering our pain that we neglect what is still ours to do. Your history may explain where you are now. It doesn't get to decide how you live from here. Yaakov couldn't undo what had happened in his father's household, but he still had a journey to finish.

A vow, and a long road

The aliyah closes with Yaakov's vow. He speaks of Elohim being with him, guarding him on the way, giving him food to eat and clothing to wear, and bringing him back to his father's house in peace. And he pledges a tenth of everything he is given.

Readers have long argued over what Yaakov's conditional wording means. Is he hedging his bets, or solemnly answering the promise he has just heard? The text doesn't settle it, and I'd rather leave the question open than pass judgment on his heart.

What isn't open to debate is that Yaakov leaves Beit-El with a promise and a life still unfinished. Haran is ahead of him. So are love and disappointment, marriage and children, and years of difficult dealings with Lavan. The encounter didn't remove the road. He still had to walk it.

I think this is where the aliyah presses hardest on us. It's easy to long for a dramatic encounter and lose sight of what faithfulness looks like on an ordinary weekday: keeping your word, dealing fairly, refusing to deceive, looking after the people entrusted to you, and doing the next right thing while the future stays unclear. None of that becomes less important after a powerful spiritual experience. If anything, it matters more.

Getting up from Beit-El

Recognising יהוה in an extraordinary moment may turn out to be the easier test. The harder one is whether we stay faithful to His instruction once the moment has passed.

Yaakov woke up, set up the stone and went on his way. He couldn't stay at Beit-El, and we can't live on yesterday's encounter either. Sooner or later we have to get up and walk, with some of our questions still unanswered and the next chapter no easier than the last, because obedience can't be postponed until life becomes comfortable.

So before today is over, find one place where you've been using uncertainty as a reason to put off what you already know is right. It might be something you said that needs correcting, an honest conversation you owe someone, or a responsibility you've let slide while you wait for things to improve. Don't wait for a better moment to be faithful. Be faithful in the one you have.

Yaakov lay down with a stone under his head and an uncertain road in front of him, and he got up with a promise. The stone was never the end of his story, and Beit-El was never meant to excuse him from the road that followed.

Boker Tov, mishpachah.
